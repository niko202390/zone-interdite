# German security, manhunts and Enigma in occupied France, 1943–44

Research notes for the SOE stealth/survival FPS (north Brittany, winter 1943 to early 1944).

**Tags:** [solid] = two or more independent good sources agree. [single-source] = one source (often one Wikipedia article). [uncertain] = conflicting, weakly sourced, or not confirmed.

**Caveat:** These notes rely mostly on Wikipedia (EN/FR/DE) and the pages it cites, plus Crypto Museum, Dirk Rijmenants' cipher site, and the US War Department *Handbook on German Military Forces* (TM-E 30-451, 1945). Before anything goes into in-game text, check it against M.R.D. Foot's *SOE in France*, Leo Marks' *Between Silk and Cyanide*, and Lucien Dumais' *The Man Who Went Back*.

## 1. Who did what (organisations and identifiers)

**Wehrmacht garrison**
- North Brittany came under LXXIV Corps (HQ Guingamp). The rest of Brittany came under XXV Corps (HQ Pontivy); in February 1944 it held the 275th, 265th and 343rd Infantry Divisions. [single-source]
- The 266th Infantry Division was a static division formed on 20 May 1943 and in Brittany by 1 August 1943. Its regiments had two battalions each and its guns were captured Soviet howitzers. [single-source]
- 1944 fortress garrisons: Brest 38,000, Lorient 25,000, Saint-Malo 12,000. [single-source]

**Feldgendarmerie (military police)**
- Duties: traffic control, checking passes, rear-area control, hunting deserters and stragglers, anti-partisan work. [solid]
- Strength: Trupps of about 30–40 men; a divisional Trupp had up to 3 officers, 41 NCOs and 20 men. [solid]
- **Identifier:** a metal gorget on a chain, inscribed *Feldgendarmerie*. The Army/SS version had a standing eagle, the Luftwaffe version a flying eagle. Branch colour was orange. Nicknames: *Kettenhunde* and *Heldenklauer* ("hero-snatchers"). [solid]
- The popular claim that the gorget was luminous for night duty is unsourced. [uncertain]
- One article says the Feldjägerkorps "superseded" the Feldgendarmerie in January 1944. [single-source, under-cited article]

**Geheime Feldpolizei (GFP)**
- The Wehrmacht's secret field police, formed July 1939 from Kripo detectives. It acted as the Abwehr's executive arm against the Resistance and SOE. [single-source]
- Agents worked in plain clothes or uniform, could pass roadblocks, use military communications and commandeer vehicles. A unit was about 50 men. [single-source]
- At Brest the GFP arrested the Élie network on 30 April 1941. [single-source]

**Abwehr**
- Section III (counter-intelligence) ran *Funkspiel*, playing captured SOE radios back to London. [single-source]
- Hitler abolished the Abwehr on **18 February 1944** and its functions passed to the RSHA. [solid]

**Sipo/SD ("Gestapo")**
- Carl Oberg became Higher SS and Police Leader on 5 May 1942 and commanded all German police in France. He ordered mass hostage executions. [single-source]
- The SD's Paris HQ was 84 avenue Foch, and the SD in Rennes ran Bezen Perrot. [single-source]
- **Identifiers:** SS-style field grey, a plain black right collar patch, and the **SD diamond on the lower left sleeve**. Many officers worked in plain clothes. [solid]

**Kriegsmarine**
- Brest was a major U-boat and surface-ship base (U-boat pens in use from September 1941) with 333 flak guns by August 1941. [single-source]

**Vichy police and gendarmerie**
- The GMR (mobile reserve groups) were created in April 1941 in units of up to 220, wore police uniform, and joined anti-maquis drives from autumn 1943. [single-source]
- Gendarmes enforced black-market controls in Brittany in 1941. [single-source]
- In January 1944 the Quimper office of the STO (forced labour service) was sabotaged and 44,000 files destroyed. [single-source]

**Milice**
- Founded 30 January 1943. It was confined to the south until **27 January 1944**. [solid]
- A Franc-Garde unit was at Rennes in June/July 1944, interrogating at the Saint-Méen asylum and rounding up the Broualan maquis on 7 July. [single-source]
- **Identifiers:** blue jacket, brown shirt, wide blue beret, and a gamma (γ) badge. [solid]

**Bezen Perrot**
- Formed 15 December 1943 from Breton nationalists, under SD supervision, HQ 7 rue de Vincennes, Rennes. Strength was 33 to 80 men, in plain clothes until March 1944 and SS uniform after. [single-source]
- They guarded sites from January 1944 and helped arrest 37 suspected maquisards on 7 February 1944. Because they spoke **Breton**, they could penetrate networks that used it. [single-source]

**Informers**
- By 1942 the Paris Kommandantur was receiving about 1,500 poison-pen letters (the source gives no time period). [single-source]
- Brest's mayor (February 1942) and Source K's Robert Keller (December 1942) were both arrested after anonymous denunciations. [single-source]

### Gameplay hooks
- **Read the silhouette:**
  - Feldgendarmerie: gorget glints under a torch.
  - GFP/SD: plain clothes, so you can only read them by behaviour.
  - Gendarme/GMR: kepi.
  - Bezen Perrot: civilian clothes and Breton speech.
- **Breton isn't cover:** speaking Breton in front of Bezen Perrot doesn't protect you.
- **Abwehr dissolved (18 February 1944):** a story beat. The SD takes over and the hunt gets harsher.
- **Village informer meter:** denunciation letters are slow and delayed, but real.

## 2. Communications and alarm speed

**Wire**
- **Feldfernsprecher 33 (FF33)** field telephone: 8.5×11×4 in, 12 lb, 1.5 V battery, hand-cranked magneto ringing. [solid]
- Switchboards [single-source]:
  - 10-line field exchange, 20 lb.
  - Large field switchboards of 60–300 lines, with a "night alarm."
  - OB 36 fortress switchboard (30 lines) for bunkers.
- Field wire carries speech about 10 miles. [single-source]
- Teleprinters ran directly over telephone lines. [single-source]

**Radio**
- Torn.Fu.d2 VHF voice set: about 3 km voice / 10 km Morse, worked by two men on the move. [single-source]
- Torn.Fu.a2: regiment to battalion, 4 mi voice, 37 lb. [single-source]
- Feldfu.b patrol set: about ¾ mile, 28 lb. [single-source]

**Staff**
- Women auxiliaries worked telephones and teleprinters from summer 1940; about 500,000 Helferinnen of all kinds served by 1945. [solid]
- Training lasted at most 12 weeks. [single-source]

**The French PTT network**
- The Germans carried their own traffic on French long-distance cables. [single-source]
- **Source K:** PTT engineers under Robert Keller tapped the Paris–Metz cable on 18–19 April 1942, giving access to 70 Paris–Berlin circuits used by the Kriegsmarine, Luftwaffe, Wehrmacht and Gestapo. [single-source]
  - The amplifiers had high input impedance, so the Germans could not detect the taps. [single-source]
  - The Germans shut it down on 24 December 1942. [single-source]
- **Plan Violet**, activated 5 June 1944, cut underground telephone and teleprinter cables, forcing the Germans onto radio that Bletchley could read. [solid]

**Alarms**
- Air-raid sirens: Brest had about 480–600 alerts between 1940 and 1944. [solid]
- No source found for flare colours or sentry bells. [uncertain]

**Alarm speed**
- I found no source with sentry-to-region timings. [uncertain]
- The only hard figure: DF stations had to share bearings **within 3 minutes** of an agent starting to transmit. [single-source]

### Gameplay hooks
These are design inference, not documented procedure:
- **Physical alarm chain:**
  1. Sentry runs to the post.
  2. FF33 crank call to the company switchboard.
  3. Helferin patches the call through to battalion or the Kommandantur, over field wire or PTT lines.
  4. Feldgendarmerie or troops are dispatched.
- **Every link can be attacked:** cut field wire, sabotage a PTT junction, distract the board operator. The FF33's crank is audible.
- **Wreck the post's radio** (28–37 lb, so there's usually only one) to force them to use a motorcycle runner.
- **Source K-style wiretap side mission:** read patrol orders off a PTT line.

## 3. Radio direction-finding (DF) against agents

**Organisation**
- The Funkabwehr was set up in 1940 under the OKW, with a Paris office (Aussenstelle) from 1940. The Ordnungspolizei ran parallel DF units. [single-source]
- Long-range DF stations included Brest. Another source lists stations in Paris, Brittany, Augsburg and Nuremberg that could fix a set "within 16 kilometres" before a van was sent. [solid]

**Long-range stage**
- Bearings had to be shared within 3 minutes. [single-source]
- The VHF Type D set could fix a transmitter to within 1 km at 60 km. [single-source]
- Teams pre-positioned for an agent's known transmission schedule. [single-source]

**Close-range stage**
- Teams searched in patterns and got out of the car short of the target. In a small village, a van driving past could often pick out the house. [single-source]
- Vans were disguised as taxis, coal lorries or milk wagons. [single-source]
- **Belt DF** was worn under a coat: range under 400 m, with a 180° direction ambiguity. There were also suitcase-, violin-case- and bicycle-mounted sets. [single-source]

**Air DF**
- Fieseler Storch teams flew around a signal, not over it. They were mostly used in the East. [single-source]

**Results**
- The Germans claimed about 550 arrests over four years. [single-source]
- Brest, 22 March 1944: a search for clandestine transmitters ended with many arrests. [single-source]

**Power-cut technique:** not found in any source checked. [uncertain]

**SOE side**
- **B Mk II suitcase set:** 32 lb, about 20 W, 3–16 MHz. It ran on mains *or* a 6 V battery. [solid]
- **Operating rules:**
  - Stay on air no more than about 20 minutes. [solid]
  - Move houses often; Cormeau's limit was 3 days. [single-source]
  - Prefer battery power. [single-source]
  - Disguise aerials as washing lines. [single-source]
  - Key fast: Cormeau sent 18–22 words per minute against an average of 12. [single-source]
- **Ciphers on silk**, which doesn't rustle when you're searched. Every message carried security checks. [single-source]
- **Failures:**
  - Noor kept copies of every message, which let the Germans imitate her. [single-source]
  - Macalister withheld his security checks, so SOE spotted the fake messages. [single-source]
- **Survival:** the "six weeks" figure is [uncertain]. Cormeau lasted 13 months and sent over 400 messages. Noor landed 16/17 June 1943 and was arrested around 13 October 1943.

### Gameplay hooks
- **Three-stage hunt for each transmission:**
  1. A 16 km triangulation circle forms within minutes.
  2. A disguised van sweeps it.
  3. A belt-DF man on foot closes the last 400 m, and may start off in the wrong direction (the 180° ambiguity).
- **Soft cap of about 20 minutes on air.** Faster keying is an upgrade.
- **Known schedules:** the Germans pre-position for your next scheduled slot, so changing time and place matters.
- **Power trade-off:** mains is unlimited but ties you to a house; battery is limited but portable.
- **Message copies:** keeping them is a capture liability, as it was for Noor.

## 4. Population control

**Identity papers**
- The *Carte d'identité de Français* was compulsory for everyone over 16 under the law of 27 October 1940. A new numbered model began rolling out in some departments in July 1943. [single-source]

**Passes**
- An *Ausweis* or *Passierschein* was hard to obtain. [solid]
- The coastal *zone interdite* was 10 km wide from April 1941 (FR Wikipedia) or 20 km (EN Wikipedia). [uncertain on width]
- Brest was inside the zone from June 1940, and an Ausweis was needed just to enter or leave the city. [single-source]
- The escape line forged identity cards and "special passes for the forbidden coastal zones." [solid]

**Curfew**
- Being out at night without an Ausweis meant arrest. [solid]
- **Brest:** 23:00–05:00 from June 1940. It was tightened to **20:00–06:00 from 11 September 1941 as collective punishment** for residents' "rebellious attitude." [single-source]
- **Plogastel-Saint-Germain:** 21:00 curfew during Atlantic Wall construction. [single-source]
- Hours varied by town; I found no single figure for Brittany. [uncertain]

**Rationing**
- Categories were E, J1, J2, J3, A, V, T and C (by age, plus workers and farmers). Adults got about 1,000–1,500 kcal a day. [single-source]
- Black-market penalties: 2 months to 2 years in prison and fines of 16,000–100,000 F. [single-source]
- Brittany was heavily affected by German purchasing. [single-source]

**Vehicles**
- Fuel was scarce, so civilian vehicles ran on wood-gas generators (gazogène). [single-source]
- Bicycle restrictions: not confirmed. [uncertain]

**Rewards**
- The Germans offered "substantial rewards" for betraying airmen. [single-source]
- Noor was allegedly sold for 100,000 F. [single-source]

**Reprisals**
- After Feldkommandant Hotz was killed in Nantes on 20 October 1941, 48 hostages were shot on 22 October: 27 at Châteaubriant, 16 at Nantes, 5 in Paris. [solid]
- August 1944: troops of the 266th Division killed 15 civilians taken from Saint-Pol-de-Léon. [single-source]

### Gameplay hooks
- **Paper stack:** ID card, ration card (its category must match your cover's age and job), and a coastal-zone Ausweis. Each can fail separately at a checkpoint.
- **Dynamic curfew:** a loud incident moves the town curfew from 23:00 to 20:00, as in Brest in 1941.
- **Reprisal counter:** violence near a town costs civilian lives, not only alert level.
- **Black-market barter:** butter and tobacco work as currency.

## 5. Manhunts, coast defences and the sea pickup

**Checkpoints: Dhuizon, 21 June 1943**
- At a checkpoint, everyone was ordered out of the car for a papers check. The two French agents passed; the Canadians drew suspicion. [solid]
- The driver sped off, was chased and shot at by a German car, and the group was caught at **another checkpoint about 10 km away**. [solid]

**Searches**
- Bezen Perrot took part in 14 counter-insurgency operations between May and July 1944. [single-source]
- Routine patrol sizes: not found. [uncertain]

**Dogs**
- German Shepherds were used as guard and messenger dogs. [single-source]
- No source found on dogs tracking fugitives in France. [uncertain]
- But evaders on the Plouha cliff path were told to **walk in streams to throw off dogs**. [single-source]

**Vehicles**
- Kübelwagen: 80 km/h top speed, 4 seats, can crawl at 4 km/h to match marching troops. [single-source]
- BMW R75: motorcycle whose sidecar wheel is also driven. [single-source]

**Aircraft**
- Fieseler Storch: stalls at 50 km/h, lands in about 30 m, cruises at 130 km/h. [single-source]
- Use as a spotter in Brittany: unconfirmed. [uncertain]

**Atlantic Wall**
- Rommel's programme from early 1944 added mined beach obstacles and *Rommelspargel* ("Rommel's asparagus") anti-landing stakes, and almost 6 million mines in northern France by D-Day. [single-source]
- S-Mine 35: tripwire trips at a 9–13 lb pull; it jumps 3–5 ft and fires about 360 steel balls. [single-source]

**Plage Bonaparte (Plouha)**
- Steep cliffs with an artillery blockhouse 1,200 m north. A bunker overlooked the cove but never noticed the operations. [solid]
- **17 mines** were found and marked on the beach. [solid]
- The approach was about 2 km of mined cliff path. [single-source]

**Shelburne line (MI9, not SOE), run by two French-Canadians**
- **Lucien Dumais**: Montreal, Fusiliers Mont-Royal, escaped within hours of capture at Dieppe; alias "Léon." **Raymond LaBrosse** was his radio operator. [solid]
- They landed on 19 November 1943. The first evacuation was on 28 January 1944. [solid]
- Five runs between January and March 1944 carried 118 aircrew. The total by boat was about 145, with the last run on 9 August 1944. [solid]
- No member was caught. [single-source]

**How a pickup worked**
1. Paris helpers supplied forged papers (with Breton names), clothes and coastal passes. [solid]
2. Evaders travelled by train to Plouha **during the moonless part of the month**. [solid]
3. They hid in safe houses and gathered at the "maison d'Alphonse." [solid]
4. A BBC message ("Bonjour à tous dans la maison d'Alphonse") announced a pickup for the next night. [single-source]
5. They walked single file, each holding the coat ahead, in silence with no smoking, then slid down the cliff and waded in waist-deep. [single-source]
6. The motor gunboat waited unlit about 3 km out for several hours while sailors rowed in. The crossing took about 4 hours, to Dartmouth or Plymouth (sources differ). [uncertain on port]
7. Signal lamps or Morse codes from the beach: not confirmed. [uncertain]

**SOE sea runs nearby**
- SOE had a Helford small-boat flotilla of motor gunboats and torpedo boats. [single-source]
- OVERCLOUD (from October 1941) ran Helford to St-Pabu in Finistère. [single-source]
- SOE's RF Section landed agents near Beg-an-Fry on 17 and 21 March 1944. [single-source]
- Air drops needed a full moon; sea pickups needed dark nights. [solid]

### Gameplay hooks
- **Checkpoint chain:** running one checkpoint alerts the next, about 10 km on.
- **Moon calendar:** pickups only happen in the dark phase. Miss it and you hide about 3 more weeks.
- **BBC message:** listen at set hours for the "maison d'Alphonse" phrase.
- **Finale:**
  - Single file on a mined cliff path, no lights.
  - A blockhouse 1.2 km away.
  - Streams break your scent trail for the dogs.
  - The boat waits only a fixed time.
- **Storch:** a rare daytime threat, not a constant one.

## 6. Enigma at a station

**Models**
- **Heer/Luftwaffe:** Enigma I. Three rotors in use from a set of 5, 10 plugboard cables, 4.5 V battery, oak case. About 28×34×15 cm and about 12 kg. [solid; weight single-source]
- **Kriegsmarine:** M3, plus the four-rotor M4 for U-boats from 1 February 1942. Other naval users ran the M4 in 3-rotor mode. [solid]
- The Abwehr used its own model, the Enigma G. [single-source]

**Key sheets (Heer/Luftwaffe)**
- One sheet covered a month, one row per day. Rows ran in reverse date order, so each day's strip could be cut off and **destroyed after use**. [solid]
- Most keys changed every 24 hours. The changeover hour is not confirmed. [uncertain]

**Kriegsmarine**
- Inner settings changed on odd days, outer settings daily. [single-source]
- Key sheets were printed in **red water-soluble ink on pink paper**. [solid]
- Ships likely to be captured did not carry Enigma. [single-source]

**Operating**
- Two operators: one types, one writes down the lit letters. The text is then sent in Morse. [solid]
- Heer/Luftwaffe messages were capped at 250 letters, in 5-letter groups. [solid]
- An officer set the rotor order and could lock the front panel. [single-source]

**Storage**
- *Lauenburg* kept next month's keys "locked in a safe." [single-source]
- How a Brittany shore station guarded its machine and keys is not documented. [uncertain]
- Retreating units sometimes blew their machines up. [single-source]

**Pinches**
- *Krebs* (4 March 1941): full February keys captured. [solid]
- *U-110* (9 May 1941): sunk under tow, and the capture stayed secret for 7 months. [solid]
- *Lauenburg* (28 June 1941): boarded, then sunk. The radio report left out the boarding "so that listening Germans would not suspect." [solid]
- The Admiralty avoided sinking too many ships in one week, to avoid raising suspicion. [single-source]
- **Land captures in France:** none found. [uncertain]

### Gameplay hooks
- **The key sheet is the real prize; the machine is a burden.** It weighs 12 kg.
- **Undetected theft pays more:** if the Germans don't know, they won't change the keys. Photograph the sheet, or stage a fire to cover it (*Lauenburg* logic).
- **Naval key sheets dissolve:** you need a waterproof pouch if you swim.
- **Two operators** means two witnesses. The officer holds the panel key.
- **The haul loses value over time:** each day's strip is destroyed, so only the remaining days of the month are worth anything.

## 7. SOE tradecraft and Canadians in F Section

**Scale**
- About 470 F Section agents served in France. The Valençay memorial lists 91 men and 13 women who died. 31 of 60 networks were destroyed by the Germans. [solid]

**Training**
- Wanborough Manor, then Arisaig (close combat), then Ringway (parachute), then the **Beaulieu** "finishing school" for security and tradecraft. [solid]

**Camp X (STS 103)**
- Near Whitby, Ontario. Opened 6 December 1941 and trained between 500 and 2,000 agents. Biéler trained there. [single-source]

**Papers and clothing**
- Station XIV at Briggens forged documents. Station XV supplied authentic French clothing. [single-source]
- Cover jobs were chosen to justify moving around; Cormeau was a "district nurse." [single-source]

**Kit** [single-source]
- Welrod silenced pistol, Sten gun, Fairbairn–Sykes knife.
- B Mk II radio (32 lb).
- Ciphers on silk; cyanide pills disguised as buttons.
- S-Phone voice link to aircraft or boats.

**Canadians**
- **Guy Biéler**: Montreal. Led the *Musician* network around Saint-Quentin. Arrested 14 January 1944, shot at Flossenbürg 5 September 1944. [single-source]
- **Frank Pickersgill** and **Ken Macalister**: both anglophone. Dropped 15/16 June 1943, arrested at Dhuizon on 21 June, killed at Buchenwald around 14 September 1944. [solid]
- **Roméo Sabourin** (killed 1944), **Alcide Beauregard** (killed at Saint-Genis-Laval, 20 August 1944), **Guy D'Artois** (survived), **Paul-Émile Labelle**, **Pierre Chassé**. [single-source]

**Accent**
- No source found naming the Québécois accent as a cover risk. [uncertain]
- The Dhuizon suspicion was about *anglophones'* non-native French. [single-source]
- Inverse case: Albert Guérisse claimed to be French-Canadian to explain his accent in English. [single-source]

### Gameplay hooks
- **Accent as a stat:** long conversations raise suspicion. A cover job gives you a reason to keep conversations short. (Design choice, not documented history.)
- **Loadout:** silk codes, a button pill, Welrod, and the 32 lb suitcase radio, which is conspicuous on trains.
- **Funkspiel:** if you're captured, your security check is how London tells it's not you.
- **Camp X and Beaulieu flashbacks** as tutorials.
